Book 1 · Preview

finding joy

by Arjun Jain

102 miles per hour. My fastest one yet. The crowd erupted. Sinclair sighed.

"Game, first set, Yousafi."

I admired the one item that always released the accumulating anxiety that naturally haunted me during matches. My racket. The faded grip, which had remained by my side since I began my ambitious endeavor, stared back at me with encouragement. The vibrant strings - dyed with yellow from the hundreds of tennis balls it endured with me - whispered tales of previous victories and losses. The injured racket frame, lacerated but loyal, had survived it all with me. My partner. My weapon. My everything.

I exhaustedly sunk into my bench where I was instantly greeted by a ball boy holding an umbrella to protect me from the piercing sun that smiled down on the vividly colored court. I glanced at my opponent next to me. She was in an animated conversation with one of her coaches. It seemed as if they were arguing. I pushed that thought to the back of my head. Not now.

As I jogged back to begin the second set, my body language changed. It was so slight that I couldn't even tell. But something was about to change. My thoughts drifted. My breaths shortened. Everything was deteriorating. And then it happened. I missed a vitally important shot and the first game of the set flew out of my grasp. I let out a frustrated sigh. Something had to change. Immediately.

I soon learnt that one singular shot was enough to have a detrimental impact for me and after just 34 minutes, I was down 5-1 in the second set. Mental stability is just as important as physical strength so I let my ambition drive me. I endured the pain. I fought harder and harder. But it was not enough. 6-4.

"Game, second set, Sinclair"

It all came down to this. The third set. The decider. Who will progress and who will be knocked out? I let my motivation carry me as I sailed through the first game with ease. Then my opponent strengthened. The rallies lengthened. The crowd's excitement grew. It grew bigger and bigger until an explosion.

"Game, Yousafi"

The second game, a 27-minute thriller, had come to an end after deuces and exhaustion. My arms hurt. My legs ached. I didn't stop.

I walked onto the court with a renewed sense of energy and drive after dominating the first game and finishing a high-stakes second game with a 2-0 lead. I was 4 games away from athletic glory. Just 4.

Winning a professional tennis match is easier said than done. Sinclair kept pushing after a horrible second game loss and she claimed the third game of the set. Then she got the fourth. The score was tied at 2-2. We were both ruthless on the court. Merciless. Unforgiving. It was a full-blown battle for the triumph. At this stage of the game, every point mattered.

Another close fight for the fifth game ended up with my opponent emerging on the top, but I clutched my mother's medallion and pressed it to my lips. The cold metal was both refreshing and empowering. It was a signal from home to keep going. A signal from Farah. My family. I felt a rush of adrenaline and hope.

3-2. I thought of everything that was riding on this. Joy. Victory. Hope. It was just enough to carry me through the first point of the game. But Sinclair had other plans and she began to dominate the sixth game and before long, she snatched it from my reach and the seventh game started. I was lost.

At 4-2, it seemed as if any dreams of triumph had escaped my mind and I started to worry. That was never a good sign. What felt so close had never been farther away. After almost 2 hours of attacking the ball, I was losing. The anxiety that diluted any other thoughts in my head ensured that the seventh and possibly the penultimate game was given to Sinclair. 5-2.

One last chance. One last fight. One last battle. One last hope. 5 consecutive games were acquired by Sinclair and only one last spark of ambition powered my body. But then I won the first point of the game. I immediately felt something different. Something… special. Almost strengthening. I began to rip continuous forehands into the deep corners of the court. My body felt power that it had never felt before. Power I never knew I had.

Keep going.

I finally claimed the game I needed. I delayed any chances of the match ending. 5-3. All the glee from the exhilaration of hitting the ball was restored and all worry that I previously felt had vanished. It was the beginning of a movie-worthy comeback.

Keep fighting.

Another game passed. My adrenaline flowed. 107 miles per hour. A personal record. The game started with excitement, followed by an ace, followed by a 5-4 score in the deciding set. The only question was: is it enough?

Just a few final games.

I only needed one final game to even the score to 5-5. My hope and anticipation went into overdrive in my head, but I did my best to keep calm and collected on the outside. I remembered what I was fighting for. And that was when I started completely pounding the ball with every ounce of strength I could muster. After 17 exhausting minutes, the score was 6-6.

Final push!

Relentlessness. Mercilessness. Pure hopefulness. I carried that through to the final battle: the tiebreaker. I felt the excitement of the crowd as well as the nervousness in my heart. Fight. Attack. Push. I thought of my family, Farah, and everything that was riding on this very match. And that is how I reached match point.

I clutched the medallion even more tightly. My hand gripped the racket with even more force. And my eyes showed wild ambition.

A bead of sweat trickled down my spine. As it dropped to the floor of the court, I hit a magnificent inside-out forehand. Vivid images of everything I stood for flickered like a light in between my mind's fear and exhaustion. I saw mādar. Pādar smiled back at me. Farah winked and smiled encouragingly. And lazy Ahmed lay sprawled across the couch, taking a nap. Finally, I saw the ball bounce on the court and remain unreturned by Sinclair. And that is how my match finished.

"Game, set, match, Yousafi"

6-2, 4-6, 7-6. A significant milestone

There was a brief pause as the world around me distorted. I could faintly hear the echoes of electric crowds, the umpire's voice, the ball kids' shoes squeaking against the court. I smiled gently before abruptly exiting the court without so much as a post-match speech.

I was greeted by a polite cheer as I walked through the locker room without even acknowledging anybody. I was waiting for the official to tell me the news.

After what felt like hours of infinite worry, one of the tournament's staff members in a light blue polo walked up to me.

"Miss Yousafi, they're waiting for you."
